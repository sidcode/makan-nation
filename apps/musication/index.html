Plate I

One tune, many homes

Mo Li Hua is Chinese, Arirang Korean, Auld Lang Syne Scottish, Amazing Grace American and Akatombo Japanese. Written out, all five use the same five notes: do re mi sol la. Move a tune into another culture's scale and listen for what stays put. Some homes turn out to be twins: Ethiopia's ambassel and Japan's in scale share every note.

Same mode, many names

Traditions thousands of kilometres apart landed on the same seven-note sets and named them differently. Pick a mode to hear it and see every name it goes by.

Twelve notes, sixty thousand songs a day

In 2022 Ed Sheeran argued that pop runs on only so many notes and very few chords, so songs are bound to bump into each other. Here is that argument, taken apart and made playable.

Plate IV

Record DNA

Pick two recordings from different decades or different places. We listen to each 30 second preview and draw its fingerprint: how fast, how bright, how loud over time, and which notes it leans on.

Tempo, key and brightness are estimates from a short clip. Your ears outrank our maths.

How to read the wheel. One lap is one octave. Distances are in cents: 100 cents is one piano semitone. Two notes rhyme here when they sit within 25 cents of each other. Gamelan and maqam tunings change from ensemble to ensemble and region to region, so treat those dots as textbook sketches.

How to read the strip. Plate II lays one octave out left to right. Blue dots sit on piano keys, pink ones fall between them, and the numbers on the arcs are step sizes in semitones.
