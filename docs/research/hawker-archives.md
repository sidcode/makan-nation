# Hawker archives and food-culture notes

Research note for Makan Nation. Compiled 7 October 2026. Centre-by-centre profiles live in `data/hawker-centre-profiles.json`; this note covers where the source material lives, short facts the game can show as tips or recipe book notes, and how hawker centres have looked over time.

Everything here is paraphrased. Where two sources disagree, both are given. Anything that could not be checked is listed at the end.

A practical warning first: several Singapore government sites (NLB Infopedia, NewspaperSG, NAS Archives Online) block automated readers and only render in a normal browser. Plan to open them by hand.

---

## 1. The archives: what they hold and how to use them

### National Archives of Singapore (NAS): Archives Online

- **Where:** https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/
- **What it holds:** public records, building plans, oral history recordings, photographs, electronic records, and an audiovisual collection of films, videotapes, slides, negatives and maps ([Wikipedia: National Archives of Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archives_of_Singapore)). Archives Online also carries speeches and government press releases (for example [this 2002 press release](https://nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/2002050202.htm)).
- **Oral history:** the Oral History Centre became part of NAS when the National Archives and the Oral History Department merged in August 1993 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Archives_of_Singapore)). Interviews with hawkers, market workers and food sellers are a good source of voice lines and everyday detail; search Archives Online for "hawker", a dish name, or a market name.
- **Photographs:** the Photographs database is searchable by keyword. Useful searches: "hawker centre", "Glutton's Square", "Orchard Road car park", "Satay Club", "Telok Ayer Market", plus each centre's name. Example record: an aerial view of hawker stalls at the Orchard Road car park in front of Cold Storage ([NAS record](https://nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/photographs/record-details/3ba3b320-1162-11e3-83d5-0050568939ad)).
- **Reuse terms** (from the [NAS photograph ordering page](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/photographs/ordering), read 7 October 2026):
  - Images online are watermarked and shown "for the purpose of research and reference". Unauthorised use or reproduction is not permitted.
  - Each record shows a "conditions governing access" line. The common one is "Viewing permitted. Use and reproduction only with permission." Others require written permission from a named copyright owner (for example SPH or the National Museum of Singapore), or say no reproduction at all.
  - To use an image, submit a request through the NAS e-Request system. NAS grants a non-exclusive, non-transferable right of use. The Request Administration Fee is S$24.45 per record including GST.
  - Users must show the credit line NAS provides, must not alter the image, and must destroy copies after the declared use.
  - **For the game:** treat NAS photos as reference for artists only. Do not trace, crop into textures, or ship them without a licence, because the terms forbid alteration.

### National Library Board (NLB)

- **Infopedia** (articles now live at `https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=...`; the old `eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_...` addresses and the old index page lead to "Page Not Found", checked 9 October 2026): NLB's encyclopaedia of Singapore topics, with short referenced articles. Relevant entries include Glutton's Square ([link](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=e429dca6-7de5-4d07-acab-cf10ca3c1647)), the Satay Club ([link](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=0d2d4bea-9182-4ac5-8d8a-041fad2a9d31)), Hainanese chicken rice ([link](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=ceddd346-4072-4981-b20d-f771bea7dd81)), chilli crab ([link](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=9bb609ca-dcf9-49eb-9941-86ff4ec2b2c5)), fried Hokkien prawn noodles ([link](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=9a26d915-109c-494e-a373-7ed8cfcda9d9)) and Maxwell Food Centre ([link](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=6a49147f-88a4-4c0b-8aa5-d1efd1a4e04f)). Each article ends with a reference list that points back to newspapers and books. The site blocked automated reads during this research, so these were not read in full here.
- **NewspaperSG** (https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/newspapers/): digitised Singapore and Malayan newspapers in English, Chinese, Malay and Tamil, going back to the 19th century. Older issues can be read free online; recent issues of SPH titles such as The Straits Times can only be viewed on terminals inside NLB libraries. Copies of SPH and Mediacorp articles must be requested from NLB under the Copyright Act ([NewspaperSG FAQ](https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/newspapers/faq); summary taken from a search index because the page blocks automated reads). Good for opening-day reports of hawker centres and old menu prices.
- **BiblioAsia** (https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/): NLB's journal. The most useful single article for this project is Lim Tin Seng's "Hawkers: From Public Nuisance to National Icons" (2013), a dated timeline from the 1860s to 2013 ([link](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons)).
- **CuriosCity** (https://curiocity.nlb.gov.sg/): NLB digital stories. "Hawkers After Independence" covers licensing in 1966, the 1971 committee, early centres and recent schemes ([link](https://curiocity.nlb.gov.sg/digital-stories/our-hawkers-through-time/hawkers-after-independence/)).
- **Reuse:** NLB's own terms page did not load for this research. Treat NLB text as copyrighted: paraphrase and cite. Newspaper images belong to the publishers.

### National Heritage Board (NHB)

- **Roots.gov.sg** (https://www.roots.gov.sg/): NHB's heritage portal, and the easiest site to read. Three parts matter:
  - *Stories*: "The Belly of a Nation" series profiles 12 hawker centres (People's Park, Maxwell, Chinatown, Tiong Bahru, Tekka, Newton, Adam, Geylang Serai, Changi Village, Chomp Chomp, Old Airport Road, East Coast Lagoon) ([series page](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/singapore-hawker-centres)). Other useful stories: "Hawker Centres: The View from Above" ([link](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story)), "Our Hawker Culture: Built for Great Taste" on architecture ([link](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste)), and "Serving Up a Legacy" ([link](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/serving-up-a-legacy)).
  - *Collections*: museum objects with photographs and catalogue notes, such as a 1970s photo of Glutton's Square ([1192808](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1192808)), the Satay Club at the Esplanade ([1191579](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1191579)) and an enamel tingkat ([1242310](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1242310)).
  - *Intangible Cultural Heritage inventory*: entries for hawker culture (ICH-050) ([link](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/hawker-culture)), chicken rice, fish head curry, char kway teow, rojak, and the kaya and kopi breakfast. Each has a description and a reference list.
  - **Reuse terms:** the [Roots terms of use](https://www.roots.gov.sg/terms-of-use) say no part of the content may be reproduced, adapted or distributed without NHB's prior written permission. Requests go to NHB_Feedback@nhb.gov.sg and should state what you want, how it will be used, for how long, and who you are. No open licence is mentioned. National Museum images are licensed through the museum's image request service (contact listed on the NAS ordering page).
- **Our SG Heritage:** the UNESCO nomination names oursgheritage.sg as the site for the #OurHawkerCulture campaign, which collected personal stories and photos ([nomination form](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/47966-EN.doc)). In October 2026 neither oursgheritage.sg nor oursgheritage.gov.sg resolved, so treat it as offline.
- **NHB UNESCO page:** background on the nomination, community support and submission ([link](https://www.nhb.gov.sg/what-we-do/our-work/sector-development/unesco/hawker-culture-in-singapore)).
- **Places, trails and exhibitions on Roots:** the *Places* section has surveyed-site pages for some centres (for example [Maxwell Road Food Centre](https://roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/Places/surveyed-sites/maxwell-road-food-centre) and [Newton Food Centre](https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/places/surveyed-sites/newton-food-centre)). Its heritage trail pages include hawker centres as stops, such as [Whampoa Makan Place on the Balestier Heritage Trail](https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/Places/landmarks/balestier-heritage-trail-faith-film-and-food/Whampoa-Makan-Place) and the [former Archipelago Brewery on the My Queenstown Heritage Trail](https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/Places/landmarks/my-queenstown-heritage-trail/former-archipelago-brewery-company), which is near ABC Brickworks. Online exhibitions include one on the [Beach Road Army Market](https://www.roots.gov.sg/resources-landing/online-exhibitions/beach-road-army-market) above Golden Mile Food Centre. The nomination also mentions NHB community trail booklets and travelling exhibitions ([nomination form](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/47966-EN.doc)). No trail devoted only to hawker centres was found.

### UNESCO: Hawker Culture in Singapore

- **Element page:** "Hawker culture in Singapore, community dining and culinary practices in a multicultural urban context", inscribed in 2020 at the 15th session of the Intergovernmental Committee (15.COM), element number 01568 ([link](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/hawker-culture-in-singapore-community-dining-and-culinary-practices-in-a-multicultural-urban-context-01568)); decision 15.COM 8.B.6 ([link](https://ich.unesco.org/en/decisions/15.COM/8.B.6)).
- **Documents on the page:** the nomination form in English and French ([EN .doc](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/47966-EN.doc)), community consent letters ([PDF](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/44412.pdf)), an extract of Singapore's ICH inventory ([PDF](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/44410-EN.pdf)), a consent video and a promotional video. The page credits the videos to NHB, NEA and the Federation of Merchants' Associations, Singapore (FMAS).
- **How to use it:** the nomination form is the best plain summary of what hawker culture means to the state and to hawkers, with figures (about 6,000 hawkers, over 110 centres) and a bibliography of about 25 books and reports. Quote it briefly and cite; the photos and videos carry their owners' copyright.

### Singapore Memory Project (SMP)

- An NLB-led programme launched in 2011 to collect personal memories of Singapore, with an early target of five million memories by 2015. It accepted written memories, images and short videos ([BiblioAsia, 2012](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-8/issue-3/oct-dec-2012/singaporean-memories/)).
- Hawker memories (old prices, favourite stalls, centres that are gone) are a natural fit for flavour text, but they are personal accounts, so cite them as memories rather than facts.
- In October 2026 the old portal address (singaporememory.sg) did not resolve, and the 2012 address (singaporememories.gov.sg) returned an empty page. Reuse terms for contributed memories could not be checked.

## 2. Food-culture facts for tips and recipe book notes

Each fact is one sentence with a period and at least one source. Where sources differ, the fact says so and gives both versions.

### Street food before the centres (1800s to 1950s)

1. **1800s:** Migrants from China, India and the region took up street hawking because it needed little money to start, and a meal of three or four dishes could cost three cents. ([Roots: Serving Up a Legacy](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/serving-up-a-legacy))
2. **1838:** Joseph Balestier's sugarcane estate, beside what is now Balestier Road, had its first harvest, and he advertised "Singapore sugar" and molasses and made rum. ([NHB: Balestier Heritage Trail](https://roots.gov.sg/-/media/Roots/Files/balestier-heritage-trail/nhb_balestier_trail-booklet_jun2020_web.ashx))
3. **1860s:** The writer John Cameron described Singapore's walking sellers: Malays with fruit, Chinese with a mix of goods, and Indian sellers with cakes. ([BiblioAsia, 2013](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons))
4. **1901:** Jalan Ampas, near Balestier, was named after ampas tebu, the crushed sugarcane left once the sugar is extracted. ([NHB: Balestier Heritage Trail](https://roots.gov.sg/-/media/Roots/Files/balestier-heritage-trail/nhb_balestier_trail-booklet_jun2020_web.ashx))
5. **1906 to 1907:** The colonial municipality brought in its first hawker by-laws; sources differ on the year, with BiblioAsia giving 1906 and Roots giving 1907. ([BiblioAsia](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons); [Roots MUSE](https://www.roots.gov.sg/muse/articles/hawker-matters-a-history-of-hawking-in-singapore))
6. **1922:** The first purpose-built hawker shelter went up at Finlayson Green. ([BiblioAsia](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons))
7. **1928:** Hawker licences were capped at 6,000, and more shelters were built at People's Park, Balestier Road, Carnie Road, Telok Ayer Market and Queen Street. ([BiblioAsia](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons))
8. **1929 to 1930:** The new Maxwell Road market lost shoppers to street hawkers, so the authorities added cooked food stalls and dining tables. ([Roots: Maxwell Road Food Centre](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/maxwell-road-food-centre))
9. **1920s to 1950s:** Hainanese migrants, many of whom had worked as cooks and stewards in colonial homes and on European ships, opened coffee shops (kopitiam) and shaped the local style of coffee. ([Roots ICH: Kaya and Kopi](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/traditional-breakfast-of-kaya-and-kopi); [SCCC Culturepaedia](https://culturepaedia.singaporeccc.org.sg/popular-culture/the-history-and-origins-of-kopi-in-singapore/))
10. **1950s:** By one estimate, more than 80 percent of Singapore's coffee shops were owned by Hainanese and Foochow families. ([SCCC Culturepaedia](https://culturepaedia.singaporeccc.org.sg/popular-culture/the-history-and-origins-of-kopi-in-singapore/))
11. **1920s to 1950s, kopitiam era:** Traditional Hainanese-style kopi beans are roasted with sugar and margarine, about eight parts beans to two parts sugar and margarine, for around 25 minutes, with salt added halfway. ([Roots ICH: Kaya and Kopi](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/traditional-breakfast-of-kaya-and-kopi))
12. **1920s to 1950s, kopitiam breakfast:** Kaya, the spread on kopitiam toast, is an egg jam made with coconut milk and sugar; the Hainanese style is brown from caramelised sugar, while the Peranakan style is green from pandan. ([Roots ICH: Kaya and Kopi](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/traditional-breakfast-of-kaya-and-kopi))
13. **Early to mid 1900s:** Stacked tiffin carriers, called tingkat, were used to deliver meals to customers or carry workers' lunches. ([Roots collection: Tiffin carrier](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1242310))
14. **1930s to 1940s:** Some of the earliest chicken rice businesses, including Yet Con, Chin Chin Eating House and Swee Kee, opened in these decades, adapting a dish descended from Hainan's Wenchang chicken. ([Roots ICH: Chicken Rice](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/chicken-rice))
15. **1946:** Long Beach Seafood opened at the Bedok Rest House by the sea and was popular with the British Army in colonial times. ([KrisShop, 2020](https://www.krisshop.com/en/the-edit/origins-of-singapores-iconic-crab-dishes); [Tatler](https://www.tatlerasia.com/profile/long-beach-seafood))
16. **December 1946:** A government "Family Restaurant" at Maxwell Market became the first of a chain selling meals for eight cents to people left poor by the war. ([Roots: Maxwell Road Food Centre](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/maxwell-road-food-centre))
17. **1949:** M.J. Gomez, a Malayalee cook from Kerala, created fish head curry at his Sophia Road restaurant because his Chinese customers prized fish heads. ([Roots ICH: Fish Head Curry](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/fish-head-curry))
18. **Late 1940s to 1995:** The Satay Club began on Hoi How Road, moved to a field near Dhoby Ghaut in the mid-1950s and to the Esplanade in 1970, and was cleared in 1995, when 8 of its 28 stallholders moved to Clarke Quay. ([Roots collection: Satay Club at the Esplanade](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1191579); [Infopedia: Satay Club](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=0d2d4bea-9182-4ac5-8d8a-041fad2a9d31))
19. **After the Second World War:** One account says Hokkien mee began with workers frying surplus noodles from nearby factories on Rochor Road, though the dish's origins are uncertain. ([Wikipedia: Hokkien mee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokkien_mee), citing [Infopedia](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=9a26d915-109c-494e-a373-7ed8cfcda9d9))
20. **1940s to 1970s:** Tok tok mee hawkers pushed three-wheeled carts with a charcoal stove through the streets and called customers by knocking a bamboo striker. ([Roots collection: tok tok mee cart](https://www.roots.gov.sg/collection-landing/listing/1121135))
21. **1950:** The colonial government set up the Hawkers Inquiry Commission in April and received its report in September, at a time when about 20,000 hawkers worked without licences. ([BiblioAsia](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons); [Roots: Serving Up a Legacy](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/serving-up-a-legacy))
22. **1950:** The commission's figures showed hawkers were about 84 percent Chinese, 14 percent Indian and 2 percent Malay. ([Roots MUSE: Hawker Matters](https://www.roots.gov.sg/muse/articles/hawker-matters-a-history-of-hawking-in-singapore))
23. **1956:** Cher Yam Tian and her husband Lim Choo Ngee began selling crab stir-fried with bottled chilli and tomato sauce from a pushcart, which grew into Palm Beach Seafood; another widely served version is credited to chef Hooi Kok Wai in the 1960s. ([Wikipedia: Chilli crab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilli_crab); [Infopedia: Chilli crab](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=9bb609ca-dcf9-49eb-9941-86ff4ec2b2c5))
24. **1958:** Only 16 inspectors were responsible for about 30,000 hawkers. ([BiblioAsia](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons))

### Licensing and resettlement (1960s to 1980s)

25. **1 March 1966:** Hawker licensing began after independence, and about 24,000 street hawkers registered. ([NLB CuriosCity](https://curiocity.nlb.gov.sg/digital-stories/our-hawkers-through-time/hawkers-after-independence/))
26. **1966 to 1978:** Glutton's Square on Orchard Road was a car park by day and an open-air food street by night, with push-cart hawkers serving locals and tourists until it closed over hygiene concerns. ([Roots collection: Glutton's Square](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1192808); [Infopedia](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=e429dca6-7de5-4d07-acab-cf10ca3c1647))
27. **23 January 1977:** In the earliest use we found of chope for keeping seats, a New Nation columnist tells two lunch companions "I'll go first and chope the seats". ([NewspaperSG: New Nation, 23 January 1977](https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/newspapers/digitised/article/newnation19770123-1.2.57))
28. **1978:** When Glutton's Square closed, its hawkers moved to Newton Circus and Cuppage, and the square came back briefly from July 2004 to February 2005 for the Singapore Food Festival. ([Roots collection: Glutton's Square](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1192808))
29. **1960s to 1970s:** While centres were being built, hawkers were moved off main roads into back lanes, side streets, vacant land and car parks. ([Roots: Serving Up a Legacy](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/serving-up-a-legacy))
30. **1960s:** Pang Siew Ting began selling Fuzhou oyster cakes, using large clams at first instead of oysters; Seth Lui dates her stall to 1964 on Tras Street while Michelin says only the 1960s, and her family still fries each cake to order at Maxwell Food Centre. ([Seth Lui](https://sethlui.com/maxwell-fuzhou-oyster-cake-singapore/); [Michelin Guide](https://guide.michelin.com/en/article/dining-out/michelin-hawker-guide-chinatown-complex))
31. **1971:** The Hawker Centres Development Committee was formed to plan purpose-built centres. ([NLB CuriosCity](https://curiocity.nlb.gov.sg/digital-stories/our-hawkers-through-time/hawkers-after-independence/))
32. **1971:** Newton Hawker Centre opened with the idea of hawker stalls in a garden setting. ([Roots: Serving Up a Legacy](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/serving-up-a-legacy))
33. **1971 and 1979:** Choong Yee Hong started as a helper at Big Fortune Claypot Rice in Chinatown in 1971 and opened New Lucky Claypot Rice at the Margaret Drive Hawker Centre, since closed, in 1979. ([Michelin Guide: Behind the Bib](https://guide.michelin.com/en/article/dining-out/behind-the-bib-new-lucky-claypot-rice))
34. **1972:** The new Ministry of the Environment took over building hawker centres and moving street hawkers into them. ([NLB CuriosCity](https://curiocity.nlb.gov.sg/digital-stories/our-hawkers-through-time/hawkers-after-independence/))
35. **1974 to 1979:** About 54 hawker centres were built across the island in these six years. ([Roots MUSE](https://www.roots.gov.sg/muse/articles/hawker-matters-a-history-of-hawking-in-singapore); [Roots: Serving Up a Legacy](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/serving-up-a-legacy))
36. **1982:** Long Beach Seafood says it created black pepper crab that year, the year it was incorporated; Tatler says only the 1980s. ([Long Beach Seafood](https://longbeachseafood.com.sg/corporate/); [KrisShop, 2020](https://www.krisshop.com/en/the-edit/origins-of-singapores-iconic-crab-dishes); [Tatler](https://www.tatlerasia.com/profile/long-beach-seafood))
37. **1982 to 1992:** Free seating was the rule by 1982, when the Ministry of the Environment said hawkers had twice been told of it, but some stallholders still shooed off diners who bought elsewhere, and People's Park, the last holdout, only switched in 1992. ([NewspaperSG: New Nation, 23 July 1982](https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/newspapers/digitised/article/newnation19820723-1.2.23.1); [NewspaperSG: Straits Times, 14 January 1992](https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/newspapers/digitised/article/straitstimes19920114-1.2.7.1.2))
38. **1980s:** The Ministry of the Environment hired contract cleaners to clear crockery and leftover food from hawker centre tables. ([Roots: The View from Above](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story))
39. **1986:** The resettlement ended with about 18,000 street hawkers in permanent centres, the last 80 coming from China Square and Haw Par Villa; sources differ on the count, with BiblioAsia giving 113 centres and a Centre for Liveable Cities case study giving 135 built from 1971 to 1985. ([BiblioAsia](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons); [Development Asia](https://development.asia/case-study/hawker-centers-serving-affordable-food-all))

### Upgrades, new centres and recognition (1990s to today)

40. **1987 and 1998:** A point demerit system for hygiene offences began in 1987, and letter grades from A to D for stalls replaced it in 1998. ([Roots: The View from Above](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story); [BiblioAsia](https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-9/issue-3/oct-dec-2013/singapore-hawkers-national-icons))
41. **8 February 2001:** The earliest clear mention of tissue choping we found is a visitor's letter to TODAY headlined "Is anyone sitting here?", subheaded "No, but the seat's taken anyway by a tissue pack". ([NewspaperSG: TODAY, 8 February 2001](https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/newspapers/digitised/article/today20010208-1.2.12.1))
42. **2001:** The Hawker Centres Upgrading Programme began, bringing anti-slip floors, new pipes, rearranged seating and better ventilation to older centres. ([Roots: The View from Above](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story))
43. **2011:** The government restarted hawker centre building after a pause of about 26 years, and by 2016 it planned 20 new centres by 2027. ([Roots: The View from Above](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story); [Development Asia](https://development.asia/case-study/hawker-centers-serving-affordable-food-all))
44. **November 2012 to 2013:** NEA piloted tray return at nine hawker centres from November 2012 and extended it to 34 more from July 2013. ([NEA, July 2013](https://www.nea.gov.sg/media/news/news/index/tray-return-initiative-to-roll-out-to-all-hawker-centres))
45. **July 2016:** Michelin's first Singapore guide gave one star each to Hong Kong Soya Sauce Chicken Rice and Noodle in Chinatown and Hill Street Tai Hwa Pork Noodle, the first street food stalls anywhere to get a star; a plate of the chicken rice cost S$2.50. ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/singapore-street-stalls-make-dining-history-michelin-star-n615141))
46. **September 2017:** NEA said it has no rules against reserving seats, and pointed to "Don't Chope Seats" house rules at Our Tampines Hub and Tiong Bahru Market. ([Mothership, September 2017](https://mothership.sg/2017/09/after-6-complaint-letters-to-straits-times-nea-highlights-no-choping-house-rules-at-2-hawker-centres/))
47. **27 to 28 May 2018:** Uber Eats shut down in Singapore and GrabFood launched island-wide the next day, with hawker stalls on its menu. ([Eatbook: GrabFood](https://eatbook.sg/grabfood/); [TechCrunch, May 2018](https://techcrunch.com/2018/05/28/grabfood-launch-southeast-asia))
48. **2021:** The soya sauce chicken stall, by then known as Hawker Chan, was left out of the 1 September 2021 guide, while Hill Street Tai Hwa (in a coffee shop on Crawford Lane, not a hawker centre) is reported to have kept its star through the 2025 edition. ([CNN via KVIA](https://kvia.com/entertainment/cnn-style/2021/09/02/famed-singaporean-eatery-hawker-chan-loses-its-michelin-star/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_Street_Tai_Hwa_Pork_Noodle))
49. **2013 or 2018, incubation stalls:** New hawkers can rent a fitted-out stall at half the market rent for nine months, then three-quarters for six months; sources differ on the start, with NEA saying its support began in 2013 and a 2021 ministry reply saying the programme launched in 2018. ([NEA ISP](https://www.nea.gov.sg/our-services/hawker-management/programmes-and-grants/isp); [NEA, Nov 2020](https://www.nea.gov.sg/media/news/news/index/nea-to-introduce-a-new-scheme-to-facilitate-the-continued-practice-of-hawker-trade-and-transmission-of-culinary-skills-from-retiring-veteran-hawkers-to-aspiring-successors); [MSE, May 2021](https://www.mse.gov.sg/latest-news/written-reply-to-pq-on-incubation-stall-programme/))
50. **January 2020 and 2021:** NEA and SkillsFuture Singapore started the Hawkers' Development Programme (training, apprenticeship, then an incubation stall), and NEA announced a Hawkers Succession Scheme to pair retiring hawkers with successors, with a pilot from early 2021 (CuriosCity dates the scheme to 2022). ([NEA, Nov 2020](https://www.nea.gov.sg/media/news/news/index/nea-to-introduce-a-new-scheme-to-facilitate-the-continued-practice-of-hawker-trade-and-transmission-of-culinary-skills-from-retiring-veteran-hawkers-to-aspiring-successors); [NLB CuriosCity](https://curiocity.nlb.gov.sg/digital-stories/our-hawkers-through-time/hawkers-after-independence/))
51. **March 2019:** Rojak, whose name is a Malay word for a mixture, joined Singapore's intangible cultural heritage inventory; there are Chinese, Malay and Indian versions, from fruit in prawn paste to deep-fried fritters with a chilli and peanut sauce. ([Roots ICH: Rojak](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/rojak))
52. **16 December 2020:** Hawker culture became Singapore's first entry on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, after more than 850,000 pledges of support. ([NEA](https://www.nea.gov.sg/media/news/news/index/hawker-culture-is-singapore-s-first-inscription-on-unesco-s-representative-list-of-the-intangible-cultural-heritage-of-humanity); [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/hawker-culture-in-singapore-community-dining-and-culinary-practices-in-a-multicultural-urban-context-01568))
53. **2019 nomination:** The nomination counted about 6,000 hawkers in over 110 centres, said more than half were second or third generation, and cited a 2018 survey in which 90 percent agreed hawker centres bring people of all backgrounds together. ([UNESCO nomination form](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/47966-EN.doc))
54. **1 September 2021:** Clearing your own tray and table became compulsory at hawker centres after a three-month advisory period, with a written warning for a first offence and fines for repeat offenders. ([NEA, May 2021](https://www.nea.gov.sg/media/news/news/index/mandatory-for-diners-to-return-dirty-trays-crockery-and-clean-table-litter-at-hawker-centres-coffeeshops-and-food-courts))
55. **April 2024:** After tray return became compulsory, the share of diners returning trays rose from 65% in August 2021 to 93%. ([NEA, June 2024](https://www.nea.gov.sg/media/news/advisories/index/cleaner-tables-in-hawker-centres-and-coffeeshops))
56. **4 March 2026:** Deliveroo left Singapore after about 11 years, leaving GrabFood and foodpanda as the apps whose riders collect from hawker stalls. ([Eatbook, 2026](https://eatbook.sg/deliveroo-closure/); [Marketing-Interactive](https://www.marketing-interactive.com/deliveroo-to-exit-singapore-platform-to-go-offline-after-4-march))

### Extra recipe-book notes (undated, not counted in the 56 facts)

- **Chicken rice:** cooking the rice with pandan leaves is a Singapore addition, and Cantonese cooks brought in younger birds and the ice-water plunge for smoother skin. ([Roots ICH: Chicken Rice](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/chicken-rice))
- **Char kway teow:** it began as a cheap, filling meal for manual labourers, and hawkers later switched from duck eggs to chicken eggs as duck eggs became scarce. ([Roots ICH: Char Kway Teow](https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/char-kway-teow))
- **Satay:** skewered meat grilled over charcoal, served with onions, ketupat rice cakes and a spicy peanut sauce. ([Roots collection: Satay Club](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1191579))

## 3. How hawker centres look

A short guide for artists, by era. Centre-specific detail is in the JSON profiles.

### Before purpose-built centres (1800s to 1960s)

- **Street stalls:** makeshift roadside setups, with diners on wooden stools around the stall and hot food served straight from the pot. ([Roots: The View from Above](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story))
- **Car parks at night:** sites such as Glutton's Square were plain open-air car parks that filled with push-cart hawkers in the evening, after the cars left. ([Roots collection](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1192808); [NAS aerial photo record](https://nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/photographs/record-details/3ba3b320-1162-11e3-83d5-0050568939ad))
- **Colonial markets and shelters:** covered markets such as Maxwell (built 1927 to 1928) and the old Tekka market of 1915, which Roots describes as having ornate steel carving and many archways, took in cooked food stalls over time. ([Roots: Maxwell](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/maxwell-road-food-centre); [Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste))
- **The Victorian one-off:** Lau Pa Sat (Telok Ayer Market) is an octagonal cast-iron hall from 1894, with ironwork made in Glasgow and a central clock tower. It is a national monument and is now privately run, so it is not part of NEA's list. ([Infopedia: Telok Ayer Market](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=1c45187f-be24-4af9-8e9d-db5dd699352d); [Wikipedia: Lau Pa Sat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lau_Pa_Sat); [Lau Pa Sat](https://www.laupasat.sg/))

### 1970s open-sided centres

- The resettlement-era centre is a single-storey, naturally ventilated shed: a roof on columns with open sides, stalls lining the edges and a shared dining area in the middle. The UNESCO nomination still describes centres this way. ([UNESCO nomination](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/47966-EN.doc))
- Some were themed to their setting. Newton (1971) was planned as hawker stalls in a garden: seven separate blocks of eight stalls under colourful nylon roofs ([Roots: Serving Up a Legacy](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/serving-up-a-legacy); [Roots: Newton](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/Hawker-Centres/Newton-Circus-Food-Centre)). East Coast Lagoon (1978) opened with 10 butterfly-shaped roofs over the cooking stalls and 40 pyramid-shaped dining shelters by the beach ([Roots: East Coast Lagoon](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/east-coast-lagoon-food-village)).
- Small estate centres were simple and compact. Chomp Chomp (1972) had 36 built-in stalls, each with its own sink and neon light ([Roots: Chomp Chomp](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/chomp-chomp-food-centre)). Adam Road (1974) is a nine-sided ring of stalls around an open courtyard, shaded by old angsana trees ([Roots: Adam Road](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/adam-road-food-centre)).
- New-town centres often had a flat concrete roof with shops above. Old Airport Road (1972 or 1973) was built as an "Emporium-cum-Hawker Centre", with food stalls on the ground floor and shops and a post office upstairs, in the same flat-roofed style as centres in Woodlands and on Commonwealth Avenue ([Roots: Blk 51 Old Airport Road](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/blk-51-old-airport-road)).
- For period props (counters, stools, signboards, lighting), search the NAS Photographs database by centre name and decade; see section 1 for reuse limits.

### Market and food centre complexes (late 1970s to 1980s)

- In HDB estates and the old city, hawker centres were often built as part of a larger block with a wet market and sometimes shops, so a player might walk up from fish and vegetable stalls to a cooked food floor. Taman Jurong's three-storey market and food centre is one example. ([Roots: The View from Above](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story))
- Chinatown Complex, finished for Chinatown's street hawkers in 1983, stacks a wet market, shops, a food centre on level 2 and car parks above. Its designers added Chinese touches such as pavilions, arches and clay tiles to suit old Chinatown. ([Roots: Chinatown Complex](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/chinatown-complex-market-food-centre))
- Other city centres sit in the podium of an HDB block with flats directly overhead, as at Hong Lim (1978) and Albert Centre on Queen Street. (See the JSON profiles; photos on Wikimedia Commons.)
- Tekka's replacement building on Buffalo Road (dated 1980, 1981 or 1982 depending on the source) was painted in bright green, yellow, orange and blue, a nod to its mixed community; a 2023 repaint changed the front to purple with coloured stripes (see the JSON profile). ([Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste))
- Commonwealth Crescent (1969, upgraded 1998) is two storeys with bright colours, wave-shaped roofs and canvas sails meant to suggest a carnival. ([Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste))

### Upgrades and rebuilds (2001 to 2010s)

- The Hawker Centres Upgrading Programme from 2001 added anti-slip flooring, new pipes, rearranged seating and better airflow ([Roots: The View from Above](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres-the-view-from-above/story)). Later work added ramps, sheltered walkways, movable seats, central courtyards for airflow and wider spacing between tables, about 1.2 metres at Tiong Bahru ([Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste)).
- Some rebuilds took on a local theme. Geylang Serai (rebuilt 2006 to 2009) has a tiered roof like a Malay kampung house, woven baskets lining the ceiling, and a double-height interior for ventilation ([Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste)). Tiong Bahru Market (rebuilt 2004 to 2006) borrows Art Deco curves from the 1930s estate around it ([Roots: Tiong Bahru](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/hawker-centres/tiong-bahru-market-food-centre)). Newton's 2006 rebuild has clay-tiled pitched roofs modelled on the colonial houses of nearby Monk's Hill Terrace ([Roots: Newton surveyed site](https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/places/surveyed-sites/newton-food-centre)).
- East Coast Lagoon's 2002 to 2004 rebuild went for a beach resort look, later adding cabana roofs and timber tables set over sand ([Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste)).

### New centres (2010s and 2020s)

- New centres since 2011 have larger stalls, wider aisles and big slow ceiling fans (high-volume low-speed fans), with space for centralised dishwashing, automated tray return and food waste digesters. ([MSE, Feb 2021](https://www.mse.gov.sg/latest-news/written-reply-to-pq-on-long-term-plans-for-hawker-culture/))
- Kampung Admiralty (2017) stacks a hawker centre of about 900 seats into a mixed building with healthcare and housing, and uses centralised dishwashing. ([Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste))
- Pasir Ris Central (2018) uses timber, plants and raw concrete inside, stalls laid out in a fan shape, and perforated metal facade panels to pull air through, looking out over Pasir Ris Town Park. ([Roots: Built for Great Taste](https://www.roots.gov.sg/stories-landing/stories/our-hawker-culture-built-for-great-taste))
- Bukit Canberra (2021) was opened with eco-friendly features. ([NLB CuriosCity](https://curiocity.nlb.gov.sg/digital-stories/our-hawkers-through-time/hawkers-after-independence/))
